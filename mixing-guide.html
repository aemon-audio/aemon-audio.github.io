What mixing is

A mix answers one question: what should the listener notice right now? In most songs that’s the voice, then the drums, then everything else. Every move you make either helps that or gets in its way.

You have four tools, and they go in this order of power: level, tone, dynamics, space. Most problems people try to solve with a fifth plugin are level problems.

How to record vocals at home

A good take needs half the plugins. These cost nothing.

The room

Stay away from bare walls and corners. A closet full of clothes, or a duvet behind you and one behind the mic, beats an empty room with an expensive microphone. Clap once: if you hear a ring, so does the mic.

The microphone

Distance
Start at about 15 to 20 cm, a hand’s width with the thumb out. Closer is warmer and boomier. Further brings in the room.
Pop filter
Between you and the mic, or aim the mic slightly past the mouth so P and B blow beside it.
Sharp S
Turn the mic a little off to the side before you reach for a de-esser.
Headphones
Closed ones, not too loud. What leaks into the mic can’t be mixed out.

The level

Record in 24-bit. Let normal lines peak around −18 to −12 dBFS and the loudest words stay under −6. There’s no reward for recording hot, and a clipped take can’t be repaired. Do the loudest part of the song as your level test, not the intro.

The performance

  • Warm up, drink water, keep your phone on silent.
  • Take three to five full passes before you fix single lines. The energy is in the passes.
  • Double the lines that matter, and record ad-libs on their own track.
  • Keep the same distance for a whole song. Step back for shouts, lean in for whispers.
  • Record ten seconds of silence. It tells you how noisy the room is.

Set up the session

  1. Name and colour every track. Group them: drums, 808 and bass, music, lead, backing, effects.
  2. Clean up. Cut silence, fix clicks, choose takes, line up doubles. Do this before any mixing.
  3. Set the gain. Bring each track to a sensible level before the first plugin, so every plugin behaves as expected. Aim for your mix bus to peak near −6 dBFS.
  4. Check phase. If a layered kick or a doubled guitar gets thin, flip the polarity of one and keep the fuller one. Utility does both.
  5. Load one or two references: finished songs in the same style. Turn them down until they’re as loud as your mix. A reference that’s louder will always win.

The order: mixing step by step

Do it in this order and each step makes the next one smaller.

  1. BalanceFaders only. 20 minutes. It should already feel like the song.
  2. CleanCuts: rumble, boxiness, harsh spots.
  3. ControlCompression and de-essing where the level jumps.
  4. ColourSaturation and broad boosts, for character.
  5. SpaceWidth, delay and reverb.
  6. RideAutomation: lift the hook, tuck the fills.
  7. MasterA little glue, peaks, level.

On one track the usual chain is cut EQ, compressor, de-esser, saturation, boost EQ, sends. Two rules explain why. Remove what you don’t want before you compress, or the compressor reacts to it. Brighten after you compress, and watch the S when you do.

Start with the most important thing. For a song led by a voice, get the voice and the drums right together, then fit the rest around them.

How to use EQ and compression

EQ

  • Cut narrow, boost wide. Two or three dB is a lot on a vocal.
  • Sweeping a big boost makes every frequency sound bad. Only cut what bothers you with the band off.
  • If two sounds fight, cut one where the other lives. Boosting both makes it worse.
  • Use auto gain, or match the level by hand. A boost that’s louder always sounds better for ten seconds.

Compression

Threshold
Where it starts. Lower it until the loud parts move 3 to 6 dB.
Ratio
How firm. 2:1 is gentle, 4:1 is control, 8:1 and up is a hand on the lid.
Attack
Slow (20 to 40 ms) lets the hit through. Fast (1 to 5 ms) holds it down.
Release
Let it recover before the next word or hit. If you hear it breathe, make it slower.

If you can’t say what the compressor is for, take it off. Two that do 3 dB each sound more natural than one doing 6.

Lead vocal, step by step

  1. Edit. Cut the noise between lines, but leave some breaths. Pull the loudest breaths down by hand instead of gating them. Fix single loud or quiet words with clip gain, so the compressor doesn’t have to.
  2. Tune first, if you tune. Natural takes a light hand, the effect takes a fast one. Either way, set the right key.
  3. Cut EQ. Low cut at 70 to 100 Hz. If it booms, take 2 to 4 dB around 150 to 250. If it sounds like a box, 2 to 4 dB around 300 to 500. If it pokes, a dynamic band around 2.5 to 4 kHz that only acts on the loud words.
  4. Compress. First compressor: 4:1, attack about 5 to 10 ms, release 60 to 100 ms, 3 to 5 dB on the loud words. Rap often takes a second, gentler one for another 2 or 3 dB.
  5. De-ess after compression, because compression brings the S up. About 3 to 5 dB on the sharp ones. If it lisps, use less.
  6. Saturate a little. It brings a voice forward where an EQ boost would only make it bright.
  7. Boost EQ. 1 to 3 dB around 3 to 5 kHz for words, or a gentle shelf from 10 kHz for air. Not both unless it needs both. Check the S again.
  8. Delay, then reverb, on sends. An eighth or quarter note delay fills the gaps. Short plate reverb, 20 to 60 ms of pre-delay so the word comes first. Cut both below about 200 Hz and above about 8 kHz. Duck them under the voice so they show up between the words.
  9. Ride the level, line by line. This is what makes a vocal sound finished, more than any plugin.

How loud should it be?

Turn your speakers down until you can barely hear the song. You should still understand every word, and still feel the snare. If the voice disappears, bring it up. If it’s all you hear, bring it down.

Mixing doubles, stacks and ad-libs

  • Doubles: 6 to 10 dB under the lead, low cut higher, S almost gone. Let one voice say the S.
  • Hook stacks: pan in pairs, send them to one bus, compress that bus, cut a little around 2 to 4 kHz so the lead keeps the words.
  • Ad-libs: make them clearly different: filtered, wider, further back, or with a longer delay. If they sound like the lead, they fight it.
  • Throws: automate the delay send on the last word of a line. One good throw beats a delay that runs all song.

How to mix drums

  1. Choose the right sounds. A sample that fits needs two plugins. One that doesn’t won’t be saved by ten.
  2. Kick and snare first, loud enough to carry the song. Then hats, lower than you think.
  3. Kick: weight around 50 to 90 Hz, box around 250 to 400 (cut), click around 2 to 5 kHz. A compressor with a slow attack adds punch, a fast one shortens it.
  4. Snare or clap: body around 180 to 250 Hz, crack around 2 to 4 kHz, air above 8. Layer two that do different jobs, and check their phase.
  5. Hats and percussion: cut everything below 200 to 400 Hz. If they’re harsh, a dip around 7 to 9 kHz beats turning them down. Pan them a little.
  6. Drum bus: 2:1 to 4:1, attack 20 to 30 ms, 2 or 3 dB, with the side-chain low cut on so the kick doesn’t drive it. Then a touch of saturation.
  7. Clip the peaks. A clipper on the drum bus can take a few dB off the first instant of each hit and you won’t miss it. That’s headroom for the whole mix. Level match and listen for the snare going soft.
  8. Parallel: a smashed copy mixed in quietly makes drums bigger without losing the hit. Turn the Mix knob down on the compressor and you have it in one plugin.

How to mix kick, 808 and bass

The low end only has room for one thing at a time.

  • Decide who’s lowest. Either the kick owns the bottom and the 808 sits above it, or the other way round. Cut the loser there.
  • Tune the 808 to the song, and shorten notes so they don’t overlap.
  • Duck it. A compressor on the 808, driven by the kick through its side-chain, fast attack, release about 80 to 120 ms. Two or three dB is often enough.
  • Make it heard on phones. A phone can’t play 50 Hz. Saturate the lows so there’s something at 150 to 800 Hz, and the ear fills in the rest.
  • Keep it in mono below about 100 to 120 Hz.
  • Trust meters down there. Most rooms lie below 100 Hz. Compare the spectrum with your reference, and check on headphones.

Width and space

  • Kick, snare, 808 and lead voice stay in the middle. Width comes from everything else.
  • Pan first. A widener is for when panning isn’t enough.
  • Check in mono. If something vanishes, it’s too wide or out of phase.
  • Depth is level plus brightness plus reverb. To push a sound back: quieter, darker, wetter.
  • Contrast makes a hook feel wide. Keep the verse narrower and drier, and it opens by itself.
  • One or two reverbs shared by many tracks sound like one place. Ten different ones sound like ten places.

Mix bus and mastering chain

A good mix needs a small master. If you’re doing more than 2 dB of anything here, go back to the track that causes it.

1. Utility
Mono check, balance, loudness. Fix phase problems before anything else.
2. EQ
Broad moves, 1 or 2 dB. Compare with the reference.
3. Compressor or Multiband
1 to 2 dB of glue, slow attack, auto release. Multiband only if one range misbehaves.
4. Saturation
Barely there. If you can hear it, halve it.
5. Imager
Lows to the middle, a little more width on top if it needs it.
6. Clipper
1 to 3 dB off the tallest peaks, so the limiter works less.
7. Limiter
Ceiling −1 dBTP. Push until it starts to hurt, then come back 1 dB.

Mix into a light version of this chain if you like, but do your balance first.

How to check loudness: LUFS and true peak

The words

LUFS
How loud it sounds, measured the way ears hear. Integrated is the whole song, short-term the last 3 seconds, momentary the last 0.4.
True peak, dBTP
The real top of the wave, between the samples too. Leave at least 1 dB, or converters and MP3s can clip.
Dynamics
The distance between peaks and loudness. Under about 6 dB, most music starts to sound flat.

How to measure

  1. Put Utility last and set it to measure the output.
  2. Press reset and play the whole song. Integrated only means something from start to end.
  3. Read integrated and true peak. Then read short-term in the loudest hook: that’s what people feel.
  4. Do the same with your reference.

You don’t have to hit −14. Plenty of hip-hop is mastered around −10 to −8 and gets turned down, which is fine if it still has punch at that level. What isn’t fine is crushing a song to win loudness that streaming takes away again.

The honest test: match your limited master to the mix before it, and compare at the same loudness. The Limiter has a button for it. If the master only sounds better when it’s louder, it isn’t better yet.

Don’t forget your own level

Mix quietly, about as loud as a conversation, and check loud for a minute at a time. Loud monitoring flatters the bass and tires your ears.

Good habits

  • Match the level on every before and after. It’s the fastest way to get better.
  • Solo to find a problem, then fix it with everything playing.
  • Take ten minutes off every hour. Ears get used to anything.
  • Bypass every plugin once at the end. Remove the ones you don’t miss.
  • Save a new version before big changes, and note what you changed.
  • Write down three things that bother you, fix only those, stop.
  • Sleep on it. Tomorrow’s first listen is the best one you’ll get.

Questions people ask

What is the right order for plugins on a vocal?

A good start is: cut EQ, compressor, de-esser, saturation, boost EQ, then delay and reverb on sends. Cut before you compress so the compressor doesn’t react to what you’re removing, and brighten after it.

Should the de-esser go before or after the compressor?

Usually after, because compression brings the S up. If one S is so sharp that it drives the compressor, use a light one before as well.

EQ before or after compression?

Both. Take problems out before, add tone after.

How loud should a mix be before mastering?

Let it peak around −6 dBFS with nothing on the mix bus that’s only there for loudness. The exact number matters less than not clipping.

What LUFS should I master to?

Spotify plays at −14 LUFS by default, but you don’t have to hit it. Much hip-hop is around −10 to −8 and gets turned down. Make it as loud as it stays good, with true peak at −1 dBTP or lower.

How do I make an 808 heard on a phone?

Saturate its lows so it gets harmonics between about 150 and 800 Hz. The phone plays those and the ear fills in the bass.

How do I mix vocals over a two-track beat?

Turn the beat down first, then dip it 1 or 2 dB around 2 to 4 kHz where the words are, and keep the reverb ducked under the voice. Ride the beat down in busy verses instead of pushing the vocal up.

Can I mix with free plugins only?

Yes. An EQ, a compressor, a de-esser and a limiter are enough for a finished song. Aemon’s are free, complete and ask for nothing. So are many others.
